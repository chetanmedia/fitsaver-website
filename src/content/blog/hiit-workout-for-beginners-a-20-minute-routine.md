A HIIT workout for beginners should feel challenging, not punishing. High-intensity interval training means short bursts of hard work followed by short recovery breaks, and the “high intensity” part is relative to your own fitness. For a beginner, that means simple moves, a work-to-rest ratio that lets you recover, and an effort level you can sustain for the whole session. This guide gives you a 20-minute, no-equipment routine, explains how to pace it, and shows how to make it harder over the coming weeks.

## What HIIT is (and what it isn’t)

HIIT alternates periods of hard effort with periods of easier effort or rest. A round might be 30 seconds of squats followed by 15 seconds of rest, repeated across several exercises. The intervals let you work harder than you could during a steady 20-minute jog, because the breaks keep you from fading.

It isn’t about collapsing on the floor at the end. Many HIIT videos online are built for people with years of training behind them, full of box jumps and burpees. You can get the benefits of interval training without any of that.

### Why HIIT is worth doing

The [American College of Sports Medicine notes](https://www.acsm.org/news-detail/2019/08/20/featured-physical-activity-guidelines-blog-high-intensity-interval-training-for-fitness-for-health-or-both) that HIIT improves fitness and performance across age groups and has health benefits too, including improved insulin sensitivity, blood pressure and body composition. For most people, the biggest practical benefit is time: a good interval session fits into 20 minutes.

## How hard should a beginner HIIT workout feel?

Use a 0 to 10 effort scale, where 0 is sitting on the couch and 10 is an all-out sprint. The [American Council on Exercise suggests](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/pros/expert-articles/5880/a-basic-high-intensity-interval-training-routine-for-beginning-exercisers/) that beginners work at about 4 to 6 on that scale. In practice:

- You’re breathing hard but could still say a short sentence.
- Your form stays clean on every rep.
- You feel ready to go again when the rest period ends.

If you can’t keep good form, slow down. Intensity should never come at the cost of technique.

### The best work-to-rest ratio for beginners

ACE describes 30 seconds of work and 15 seconds of rest as a safe place to start. That 2:1 ratio gives you enough work to raise your heart rate and enough rest to recover between moves. As you get fitter, you can shorten the rest or lengthen the work.

## The 8 best beginner HIIT exercises

These moves need no equipment and can be done in a small space. Each has an easier and a harder option.

### 1. Bodyweight squat

Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, sit your hips back and down, then stand. Keep your chest up and your heels down.

- **Easier:** squat to a chair.
- **Harder:** add a small hop at the top.

### 2. Incline push-up

Place your hands on a bench, counter or sturdy table and lower your chest toward it, keeping your body in a straight line.

- **Easier:** use a higher surface.
- **Harder:** move to the floor.

### 3. Reverse lunge

Step one foot back, lower both knees toward 90 degrees and step back up. Alternate legs.

- **Easier:** hold a wall or chair for balance.
- **Harder:** go a little faster while staying controlled.

### 4. Marching high knees

Drive one knee up toward hip height at a time while pumping your arms. Marching keeps it low-impact.

- **Easier:** march slowly.
- **Harder:** switch to running high knees.

### 5. Step jacks

Step one foot out to the side while raising your arms overhead, step back in and repeat on the other side. It’s a jumping jack without the jump.

- **Easier:** keep arms at shoulder height.
- **Harder:** switch to regular jumping jacks.

### 6. Glute bridge

Lie on your back with knees bent, press through your heels and lift your hips, then lower.

- **Easier:** smaller range.
- **Harder:** hold the top for two seconds each rep.

### 7. Plank shoulder tap

Hold a high plank and tap one shoulder with the opposite hand, keeping your hips still.

- **Easier:** do it from your knees.
- **Harder:** bring your feet closer together.

### 8. Skater step

Step wide to one side, sweep the back leg behind you and touch the floor lightly if you can, then step to the other side.

- **Easier:** smaller steps.
- **Harder:** turn the steps into small lateral hops.

## How to warm up and cool down

Spend three to four minutes warming up before your first interval: march in place, do arm circles, a few slow squats and some hip swings. A warm-up raises your heart rate gradually and gets your joints ready to move.

After the last interval, walk around for a couple of minutes until your breathing settles, then stretch your legs, hips and chest. If you’re new to recovery work, our guide to [rest days](/blog/rest-days-what-to-do-and-how-many-you-need) explains what to do between sessions.

## How often should beginners do HIIT?

Two or three HIIT sessions a week, on non-consecutive days, is plenty when you’re starting out. Intervals are demanding, and your body adapts during the rest days between them. The [CDC recommends](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/adults.html) that adults get 150 minutes of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity each week, plus muscle-strengthening work on two or more days. A few HIIT sessions can make up a solid share of that, alongside walking and strength training.

### How to progress

Once the routine below feels manageable, change one thing at a time every week or two:

- Add a third round.
- Change the ratio from 30:15 to 40:20.
- Swap one or two moves for their harder versions.
- Cut the rest between rounds from 60 seconds to 45.

## Beginner HIIT workout routine card

Do every move for 30 seconds, rest 15 seconds, then move to the next one. Rest 60 seconds after the eighth move. Complete two rounds for a beginner session, three once you’re ready. With the warm-up and cool-down, it takes about 20 minutes.

1. **Bodyweight squat:** 30 seconds work, 15 seconds rest
2. **Incline push-up:** 30 seconds work, 15 seconds rest
3. **Reverse lunge:** 30 seconds work, 15 seconds rest
4. **Marching high knees:** 30 seconds work, 15 seconds rest
5. **Step jacks:** 30 seconds work, 15 seconds rest
6. **Glute bridge:** 30 seconds work, 15 seconds rest
7. **Plank shoulder tap:** 30 seconds work, 15 seconds rest
8. **Skater step:** 30 seconds work, 60 seconds rest, then repeat

## Common beginner HIIT mistakes

- **Going too hard too soon.** If you’re wrecked after round one, you started too fast. Aim for an effort you can repeat.
- **Choosing advanced moves.** Burpees and box jumps can wait. Simple moves done well give you the same training effect.
- **Skipping the warm-up.** Cold muscles and sudden bursts don’t mix.
- **Doing HIIT every day.** More isn’t better. Two or three sessions a week, plus walking or strength work on other days, is a better plan.
- **Sacrificing form for speed.** Count good reps, not fast ones.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is HIIT good for beginners?

Yes, as long as the moves are simple and the intensity matches your fitness. A beginner HIIT workout uses moderate effort, longer rest and low-impact exercises, then builds from there.

### How long should a beginner HIIT workout be?

About 15 to 20 minutes, including a short warm-up and cool-down, is a good starting point. The hard work itself might total only 8 to 12 minutes.

### Can I do HIIT every day?

It isn’t a good idea, especially as a beginner. Two or three sessions a week on non-consecutive days give your body time to recover. Fill the other days with walking, strength training or mobility work.

### Do I need equipment for HIIT?

No. Bodyweight moves like squats, lunges, step jacks and plank variations make a complete HIIT workout. Equipment like a bike, rower or kettlebell adds variety later.

### What should I do if I have a health condition?

If you have a heart condition, joint problems or any ongoing health concern, check with a doctor or qualified health professional before starting high-intensity training.
