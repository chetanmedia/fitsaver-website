A gym workout planner app should do one job well: tell you what to do today, then remember what you did so next week can be a little harder. Most lists of the “best” apps skip the more useful question, which is what kind of planner you need in the first place. Some people need an app to write their program for them. Some already have a program and just need a clean log. And a growing group trains from videos they found on Instagram or TikTok and needs a way to turn those clips into a plan. This guide breaks gym workout planner apps into four types, lists the features that actually matter, and explains when you can skip the app entirely.

## Do you actually need a gym workout planner app?

Plenty of people make steady progress with a paper notebook. An app earns its place on your phone when it solves a problem you actually have. Run through these questions first.

### Signs a planner app will help

- **You walk into the gym without a plan.** If you spend the first ten minutes scrolling for ideas, a planner fixes that before anything else.
- **You can’t remember last week’s weights.** Progress depends on doing a little more over time. The [ACSM position stand on resistance training](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/) describes progressive overload as necessary for continued gains and suggests a 2 to 10% load increase once you can beat your target reps by one or two. That is hard to apply if you don’t know what you lifted.
- **Your saved workouts are scattered.** Screenshots, bookmarked reels, a notes file and a PDF from a friend are four places to search mid-session.
- **You keep skipping days.** A written weekly schedule makes the plan visible, which helps more than willpower does.

### When a notes app is enough

If you run one simple program, train three days a week and already write down your weights, a notes app or notebook is fine. Use a dedicated app when the logging or the planning starts taking more effort than the training.

## The 4 types of gym workout planner apps

Most apps lean toward one of these four jobs. Some do a bit of everything, but knowing which job you care about most makes the choice simple.

### 1. Loggers and trackers

Apps like Strong and Hevy are built around fast set logging. You make routines from an exercise library, then log weight and reps during the session, and the app shows your history and personal records.

- **Best for:** People who already follow a program and want accurate numbers.
- **Watch out for:** They don’t decide what you should do. If you start with a blank screen, you’ll still need a plan from somewhere.

### 2. Program generators

Apps such as Fitbod and Planfit create workouts for you, adjusting exercise choice and volume based on your equipment, goals and recent sessions.

- **Best for:** Beginners who want structure without designing a program, and anyone bored of planning.
- **Watch out for:** Less control. If you love a particular coach’s routine, a generated plan can feel like someone else’s idea of your workout.

### 3. Library and plan apps

Apps like JEFIT combine a large exercise database with prebuilt plans you can copy and adjust, plus a log.

- **Best for:** People who like browsing ready-made splits and changing them a little.
- **Watch out for:** Big libraries can lead to endless browsing instead of training.

### 4. Video-first organizers

This is the newest group, and it exists because so many people now find workouts on social media. Instead of building routines from a list, you import a workout video and the app turns it into a routine you can run.

- **Best for:** People who train from creator videos on Instagram, TikTok, YouTube or Facebook.
- **Watch out for:** The quality of your plan depends on the videos you save, so pick creators who show clear form and sensible volume.

## Features that matter in a custom workout planner app

Whatever type you choose, these are the features worth checking before you commit. They matter far more than how many exercises the library has.

### A weekly schedule, not just a list of workouts

A list of routines is a menu. A schedule is a plan. Look for a way to assign workouts to days so you open the app and see today’s session. If you’re still deciding how many days to train, start with our [workout split guide](/blog/workout-split-guide-how-to-pick-the-right-3-4-5-or-6-day-split), then build the week around it.

### Fast logging per set

You should be able to log weight and reps for each set in a couple of taps, with the last session’s numbers visible. Slow logging is the main reason people abandon trackers.

### Rest timers and a clear “what’s next”

A built-in rest timer keeps sessions on schedule and stops you from scrolling between sets. Round-based timers matter too if you do circuits or HIIT.

### Easy editing

A custom workout planner app has to let you rename, reorder, add and remove exercises without starting over. Your program will change, so editing should take seconds.

### Organization that scales

Ten routines are easy to manage. Fifty are not. Folders or catalogs by muscle group or goal, plus search, keep your library usable as it grows.

### Something that keeps you coming back

Streaks, history and monthly stats make consistency visible. They aren’t magic, but seeing a streak you don’t want to break is a real nudge on low-energy days. And a broken streak is not a disaster: habit research summarized by the [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit) found that a single missed day had little impact on how automatic a behavior became. Our guide on [how to build a workout habit that sticks](/blog/how-to-build-a-workout-habit-that-sticks) covers why.

## How to set up any gym workout planner in 20 minutes

The app matters less than the setup. Do this once and you’ll have a plan you can follow for the next month.

1. Pick your training days. Choose a number you can keep on a busy week. The [CDC’s adult activity guidelines](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/adults.html) recommend muscle-strengthening activity on 2 or more days a week, and the ACSM suggests 2 to 3 days for beginners, so three is a strong start.
2. Choose a split. Full body for two or three days, upper/lower for four, push/pull/legs for five or six.
3. Build or import one routine per day. Four to six exercises each is plenty.
4. Set sets, reps and rest. For most beginners, 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps with 60 to 90 seconds of rest.
5. Schedule the week. Put each routine on its day, including rest days.
6. Log every set for four weeks. Then review. Add weight where you hit the top of your rep range on every set.

If you want a deeper walkthrough of step 5, read [how to create a workout schedule you will actually stick to](/blog/how-to-create-a-workout-schedule-you-will-actually-stick-to).

## A sample 3-day gym plan to put in your planner

Here is a simple full body week you can load into any planner app. Train on nonconsecutive days, such as Monday, Wednesday and Friday.

1. **Goblet squat:** 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps, 90 seconds rest
2. **Dumbbell bench press:** 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps, 90 seconds rest
3. **Seated cable row:** 3 sets of 10 to 12 reps, 60 seconds rest
4. **Romanian deadlift:** 3 sets of 8 to 10 reps, 90 seconds rest
5. **Dumbbell shoulder press:** 2 sets of 10 to 12 reps, 60 seconds rest
6. **Plank:** 3 holds of 30 to 45 seconds, 45 seconds rest

Use the same routine all three days for the first month, or swap the squat for a leg press and the bench press for a push-up variation on day two for some variety. When every set reaches 12 clean reps, increase the weight slightly.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best gym workout planner app for beginners?

The best one is the one that matches how you get your workouts. If you have no plan, a program generator removes the guesswork. If you follow a coach or creator, a video-first organizer like Fitsaver keeps their workouts in one place. If you already have a program, a simple logger is enough.

### Is a free workout planner app good enough?

Often, yes. Most planner apps offer free tiers with core logging and routines. Paid plans usually add things like unlimited imports, advanced scheduling or deeper stats. Try the free version for two weeks before paying for anything.

### Can I make a custom workout plan in an app?

Yes. Most planner apps let you build routines from an exercise list, set sets, reps and rest, and schedule them by day. Video-first apps also let you start from a workout video and then edit it.

### How many days a week should my gym plan include?

For beginners, 2 to 3 strength days a week is a solid starting point, in line with CDC and ACSM guidance. Add days only when you are recovering well and showing up consistently.

### Should I track every set?

Tracking every set is the easiest way to apply progressive overload, because you can see exactly when to add weight. If that feels like too much, at least log your heaviest set of each exercise.
