A **kettlebell ab workout** trains your core the way it actually works in real life: by bracing against a weight that wants to pull you forward, twist you sideways or drop you out of position. Because the handle sits off-center from the bell, a kettlebell is harder to control than a dumbbell of the same weight, and that extra instability is exactly what your abs, obliques and deep core muscles respond to. This guide covers the seven best kettlebell ab exercises, how to choose the right bell, a 25-minute routine card you can run two or three times a week, and simple regressions for every move.

## Why use a kettlebell for your ab workout?

Most ab routines are built around crunches and leg raises that curl your spine. But your core’s main job is stopping your torso from bending, twisting and collapsing while your arms and legs work. Kettlebells train exactly that.

- **Offset load.** The bell hangs below and away from the handle, so every carry, pass and hold forces your core to fight for position.
- **One bell, many angles.** You can load your abs from the front (dead bugs, sit-ups), the side (suitcase carries), in rotation (halos, pull-throughs) and with speed (swings).
- **Full-body payoff.** Swings and get-ups train your core alongside your hips, shoulders and grip.

There is research behind the hype, too. In an [ACE-sponsored study](https://www.acefitness.org/about-ace/press-room/press-releases/3238/ace-study-finds-kettlebell-training-effective-for-improving-more-than-strength/), young adults who did eight weeks of twice-weekly kettlebell classes showed a marked increase in core strength, along with better aerobic capacity and dynamic balance.

## How to choose your kettlebell weight

The most common mistake with a kettlebell ab workout is grabbing the same bell you swing with and using it for every move. Core exercises need very different loads.

- **Swings:** These are hip-driven, so most people can handle a moderate to heavy bell. Many beginners start around 8 to 12 kg (18 to 26 lb) for women and 12 to 16 kg (26 to 35 lb) for men.
- **Carries:** You can usually carry more than you can swing. Start with your swing bell and progress from there.
- **Overhead and floor moves (dead bugs, halos, get-ups):** Go much lighter. A 4 to 8 kg (9 to 18 lb) bell is plenty for most people while they learn the patterns.

A good rule: pick a weight you could do two or three more reps with at the end of each set without your lower back arching, your ribs flaring or your hips shifting. If form breaks, the bell is too heavy for that exercise, even if you can technically move it.

## The 7 best kettlebell ab exercises

Each move below includes the setup, the key cue, a common mistake and an easier option. Start with the regressions if you’re new to kettlebells and progress once every rep looks the same.

### 1. Kettlebell dead bug

Lie on your back holding a light kettlebell by the horns (the sides of the handle) straight above your chest. Lift your legs so your knees are bent at 90 degrees over your hips. Slowly lower one leg toward the floor while the bell stays still, then return and switch sides.

- **Cue:** Press your lower back gently into the floor and keep it there. Exhale as the leg lowers.
- **Common mistake:** Letting the lower back peel off the floor as the leg goes down. Shorten the range until you can control it.
- **Easier option:** Tap your heel to the floor instead of straightening the leg.

### 2. Plank pull-through

Get into a high plank with a kettlebell on the floor just behind your left hand. Reach under your body with your right hand, grab the handle and drag the bell across to the right side. Switch hands and repeat.

- **Cue:** Keep your hips level, as if a glass of water were balanced on your lower back.
- **Common mistake:** Rocking the hips side to side with every pull. Widen your feet for more stability.
- **Easier option:** Do it from your knees, keeping a straight line from knees to head.

### 3. Kettlebell halo

Stand tall or kneel on one knee, holding the bell upside down by the horns at chest height. Circle it around your head, keeping it close, then reverse direction after each rep.

- **Cue:** Squeeze your glutes and keep your ribs down. Your torso stays still while the bell moves.
- **Common mistake:** Leaning back as the bell passes behind your head. That turns a core exercise into a back arch.
- **Easier option:** Use a lighter bell and a smaller circle, or do it seated on a bench.

### 4. Suitcase carry

Hold one kettlebell at your side like a suitcase and walk 20 to 30 steps, then switch hands. It looks simple, but the bell constantly tries to pull you sideways, and your obliques have to stop it.

- **Cue:** Stand tall with level shoulders. Walk with short, controlled steps.
- **Common mistake:** Leaning away from the bell or letting it drift and touch your leg.
- **Progression:** Go heavier, or switch to a front-rack carry with the bell resting at your shoulder.

### 5. Two-hand kettlebell swing

Stand with feet a little wider than hip-width, the bell a foot in front of you. Hinge at the hips, hike the bell back between your legs, then drive your hips forward to float it to chest height. Let it fall back and hinge again.

- **Cue:** At the top, you should look like a standing plank: glutes squeezed, abs braced, knees locked.
- **Common mistake:** Squatting the bell up or lifting it with your arms. The power comes from your hips.
- **Easier option:** Practice the hinge with a kettlebell deadlift until the pattern feels natural.

### 6. Half Turkish get-up

Lie on your back with the bell pressed straight up in your right hand, right knee bent and right foot flat. Roll onto your left elbow, then press up onto your left hand while your eyes stay on the bell. Reverse the steps slowly to return. Do all reps on one side, then switch.

![Man performing a kettlebell get-up on a gym floor with the kettlebell locked out overhead](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1601422407692-ec4eeec1d9b3?crop=entropy&cs=srgb&fm=jpg&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&w=1600&q=80&auto=format)

- **Cue:** Keep the arm holding the bell locked and vertical the whole time.
- **Common mistake:** Rushing. Every step should be a pause, not a bounce.
- **Easier option:** Practice with no weight, or balance a shoe on your fist, until the sequence is automatic.

### 7. Kettlebell Russian twist

Sit with knees bent and heels on the floor, holding the bell by the horns at your chest. Lean back slightly with a long spine, then rotate your shoulders to tap the bell beside one hip, then the other.

- **Cue:** Rotate from your rib cage, not just your arms. The bell follows your chest.
- **Common mistake:** Rounding your back and swinging the bell side to side for speed.
- **Easier option:** Hold the bell at your chest and rotate without tapping it to the floor. If twisting bothers your back, swap in a second set of suitcase carries.

## How to program a kettlebell ab workout

Your abs recover like any other muscle group. Two or three kettlebell ab sessions a week, with at least a day between them, is plenty for most people. That fits neatly with the [CDC’s physical activity guidelines](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/adults.html), which recommend muscle-strengthening work on two or more days a week for all major muscle groups, including the abdomen.

### Sets, reps and rest

- **Slow, controlled moves (dead bugs, halos, get-ups):** 2 to 3 sets of 5 to 10 reps per side.
- **Plank pull-throughs and Russian twists:** 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps per side.
- **Swings:** 3 to 5 sets of 10 to 15 reps.
- **Carries:** 3 rounds of 20 to 30 steps per hand.
- **Rest:** 30 to 60 seconds between sets, a little longer after heavy swings.

### Progress one variable at a time

When every set looks clean at the top of the rep range, add one thing: a few more reps, a slower lowering phase, a longer carry or a heavier bell.

### Where it fits in your week

Run the routine as a finisher after a lower-body or full-body day, or on its own on a lighter day. ## Kettlebell ab workout routine card

Here’s a 20 to 25 minute kettlebell ab workout. Do it two or three times a week. Warm up first with a few minutes of easy movement, such as marching in place, cat-cow and bodyweight hip hinges.

1. **Kettlebell dead bug:** 3 sets of 8 reps per side, 45 seconds rest
2. **Plank pull-through:** 3 sets of 8 reps per side, 45 seconds rest
3. **Kettlebell halo:** 2 sets of 6 circles each direction, 30 seconds rest
4. **Suitcase carry:** 3 rounds of 25 steps per hand, 45 seconds rest
5. **Two-hand kettlebell swing:** 4 sets of 12 reps, 60 seconds rest
6. **Half Turkish get-up:** 2 sets of 3 reps per side, 60 seconds rest
7. **Kettlebell Russian twist:** 2 sets of 10 reps per side, 30 seconds rest

Short on time? Do moves 1, 4 and 5 as a 10-minute circuit. Stop any exercise that causes pain, and keep every rep slow enough that you control the bell rather than the bell controlling you.

## Common kettlebell ab workout mistakes

- **Using one weight for everything.** Swings and carries need a heavier bell than dead bugs and halos. Match the load to the move.
- **Chasing speed.** Fast, sloppy reps let momentum do the work. Control is the point of every exercise here except the swing, and even swings need a crisp, braced finish.
- **Holding your breath the whole set.** Brace, then breathe behind the brace. A sharp exhale at the top of each swing and during each dead bug lowering helps.
- **Skipping the regressions.** The easier versions aren’t a step back. They build the positions that make the harder versions safe.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you build abs with just a kettlebell?

Yes. One kettlebell can load your core from the front, the side and in rotation, which covers everything a well-rounded ab routine needs. Whether your abs are visible depends mostly on body fat, so pair the training with consistent eating and sleep habits.

### Are kettlebell swings good for your abs?

Swings train your abs to brace hard at the top of each rep, along with your glutes and hamstrings. They’re a strong addition to an ab workout, but pair them with slower moves like dead bugs and carries for balanced core training.

### What weight kettlebell should I use for ab exercises?

Use a lighter bell, often 4 to 8 kg (9 to 18 lb), for floor and overhead moves like dead bugs, halos and get-ups. Use a moderate to heavy bell for swings and carries. Choose a weight that lets you finish each set with clean form and a couple of reps to spare.

### How often should I do a kettlebell ab workout?

Two or three times a week is enough for most people. Leave at least one day between sessions so your core can recover, and progress gradually.

### Are Russian twists bad for your back?

For many healthy people, controlled twists with a long spine are fine. If twisting causes discomfort or you have a back injury, skip them and use suitcase carries or plank pull-throughs instead, and check with a qualified health professional. [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/staying-healthy/want-a-stronger-core-skip-the-sit-ups) also points to planks and other hold-style moves as a back-friendly way to train the whole core.
